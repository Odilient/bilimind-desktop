# How Malay sounds and how it is spelled

> [!info] Stuff to memorise · 15 min · study Sun 11 Oct
> You will be able to read any Malay word you meet and say it close enough to be understood. Covers Rumi spelling, the letter-to-sound table in section 1 of your phrasebook, and the final -a that softens to 'uh', as in saya. Prevents reading Malay with English vowel rules, which is what turns terima kasih into something nobody recognises.

## Explainer

Malay uses the same 26 letters you already read. The trap is that it uses them differently. English **e** has several sounds; Malay **e** has two. English **c** is sometimes *k*, sometimes *s*; Malay **c** is always the *ch* in *church*. Read *terima kasih* with English vowel rules and you get "teh-RYE-mah KAY-see" — nobody in a mamak will turn around. Read it with the table below and you get "tuh-ree-mah kah-seeh", which works.

Two rules carry most of the load.

**Vowels are short and pure.** *a* is the *a* in *father*, *i* is *ee* in *see*, *u* is *oo* in *food*, *o* is *o* in *low* with no *w* sliding off the end. No diphthongs — do not glide.

**Consonants are single-valued.** *c* is always *ch*. *g* is always hard, as in *go* — never the soft *g* of *giant*. *j* is the *j* in *jug*. *ng* is the *ng* in *singer*, with no hard *g* after it; *ngg* is the *ng* in *finger*, with one. *ny* is the *ny* in *canyon*. *sy* is *sh*. *kh* is the Scottish *ch* in *loch*.

Two endings need practice. A word-final **-a** softens to a short *uh* in everyday Peninsular speech: *saya* is "sa-yuh", not "sa-yah". And a word-final **-k** is not a *k* at all — it is a stop in the throat, the sound you make in the middle of "uh-oh". *tidak* comes out "tida'" with the mouth closing and nothing released.

Stress is light and even. Most speakers lean slightly on the second-last syllable, but leaning wrong costs you far less than mispronouncing a *c* or a final *a*.

## Flashcards

Malay **c** — as in *cakap* :: *ch* in *China*: chah-kahp. Never *k*, never *s*.
Malay **g** — as in *gula* :: Always hard, as in *go*: goo-lah. Never the soft *g* of *giant*.
Malay **ng** vs **ngg** :: *ng* = *singer*, no hard g (*dengan*). *ngg* = *finger*, one hard g (*tangga*).
Word-final **-a** :: Softens to a short *uh* in everyday Peninsular speech: *saya* = sa-yuh.
Word-final **-k** :: A stop in the throat, nothing released: *tidak* = tee-dah'.
Malay **e** — two values :: Usually short *uh* as in *about* (*besar* = buh-sar); sometimes *e* in *bed* (*meja* = meh-ja).
Malay **sy** and **kh** :: *sy* = *sh* in *shop* (*syarikat*). *kh* = Scottish *ch* in *loch* (*khabar*), often softened to *k*.
Malay **ny** :: *ny* in *canyon*: *nyanyi* = nyah-nyee.
Malay **o** :: *o* in *low*, with no *w* gliding off the end: *kopi* = ko-pee.
Malay **r** :: A tapped r, as in Spanish. Word-finally it is often dropped: *besar* can sound like buh-sa.
Where does the stress fall? :: Light and fairly even; many speakers lean slightly on the second-last syllable.
Malay **i** and **u** :: *i* = *ee* in *see* (*ikan*). *u* = *oo* in *food* (*susu*). Short and pure, no glide.

## Reading terima kasih off the table

Your phrasebook lists *terima kasih* (thank you). Here is the whole word taken apart with the table, letter group by letter group.

| Chunk | Letters | Rule | Sound |
|---|---|---|---|
| te- | t, e | **e** is the short *uh* of *about* | tuh |
| -ri- | r, i | **r** tapped; **i** is *ee* in *see* | ree |
| -ma | m, a | **a** is *a* in *father*; it ends the word *terima*, so in relaxed speech it may soften to *uh* | mah |
| ka- | k, a | same **a** | kah |
| -sih | s, i, h | **i** is *ee*; final **h** is breathed | seeh |

Put together: **tuh-ree-mah kah-seeh**. Stress sits lightly on the second-last syllable of each word — *tuh-REE-mah*, *KAH-seeh*.

Now the same method on a word you will meet at the counter. *ais* (iced) is not English "ace": **a** is *a* in *father*, **i** is *ee*, so it is "ah-ees", two syllables, not one. And *kopi* is "ko-pee", not "KOH-pye".

Run the method on any new word: split it, apply the table to each chunk, then check the two endings — a final **-a** softens, a final **-k** stops.

## A common mix-up

> [!failure] The belief
> Malay is written in the Latin alphabet, so I can just read it the way I read English.

**Why it seems right.** The letters are identical and most of the words look familiar on the page. *Meja*, *kopi*, *susu* — nothing in the spelling warns you that a rule is about to change under you.

> [!success] What is true
> Malay shares the alphabet but not the letter values. *c* is always *ch*, never *k* or *s*. *g* is always hard. Word-final *a* softens to *uh* and word-final *k* becomes a throat stop. Read *terima kasih* with English rules and you produce "teh-RYE-mah KAY-see"; read it off the table and you produce "tuh-ree-mah kah-seeh". The difference predicts whether a mamak counter understands you on the first try or the third.

How Malay sounds Malay is written in the Latin alphabet (Rumi) and is mostly spelled as it sounds. | Letter | Sounds like | Example | |---|---|---| | a | "a" in *father*; at the end of a word, often a soft "uh" in everyday Peninsular speech | *saya* (I) sounds like "sa-yuh" | | e | usually a short "uh" (like the "a" in *about*); sometimes "e" in *bed* | *besar* (big) "buh-sar"; *meja* (table) "meh-ja" | | i | "ee" in *see* | *ikan* (fish) | | o | "o" in *low*, without the "w" | *kopi* (coffee) | | u | "oo" in *food* | *susu* (milk) | | c | always "ch" in *China* | *cakap* (speak) "chah-kahp" | | g | always hard, as in *go* | *gula* (sugar) | | j | "j" in *jug* | *jalan* (road) | | …
