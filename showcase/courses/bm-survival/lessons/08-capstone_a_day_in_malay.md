# Capstone: a full day in Malay

> [!info] Something to make · 15 min · study Mon 19 Oct
> You will spend one real day doing three things in Malay: ordering at a mamak, taking a Grab and holding one conversation on campus. Covers writing your phrases out beforehand, going out and using them, and logging which lines you had to abandon for English. Prevents staying in English for the rest of the year because the first attempt felt clumsy.

## Explainer

You have the phrases. What you do not have is a day where you used them.

The failure mode this capstone prevents is specific. You order at a mamak in English, it works, and you conclude that Malay is optional. Six months later you still cannot say *teh tarik kurang manis, satu* without rehearsing it in your head first. The first attempt will feel clumsy. Clumsy is the point.

Today you run three scenes from your phrasebook in one short day:

1. **Mamak breakfast** — order a drink and food, ask for the bill.
2. **Grab ride to campus** — greet the driver, confirm the destination, thank them.
3. **One conversation on campus** — introduce yourself, ask one real question.

Before you leave, write your lines down. Not because you will read them, but because writing them commits you to a specific sentence instead of a vague intention. Use only the phrases from your phrasebook — *teh tarik kurang manis, satu*, *roti canai satu, kuah campur*, *bang, kira*, *terima kasih, bang* — spelled as your phrasebook spells them.

After each scene, log one line: which Malay phrase you actually said, and which one you abandoned for English. The abandoned ones are your real syllabus for next week.

Your phrasebook notes that final *k* is a short stop in the throat, not a full "k" — *tidak* sounds like "tida'". Say your lines out loud once before you go. Stress is light and fairly even, so do not hunt for an English-style emphasis.

## Step by step

Fifteen minutes of preparation, then you go. Do this the evening before or the morning of — not in the queue.

### 1. Pick your three scenes and write the lines

On paper or in your notes app, write three headings: **Mamak**, **Grab**, **Campus**. Under each, write the exact Malay lines you intend to say, copied from your phrasebook.

Mamak, from section 5: *Teh tarik kurang manis, satu.* Then *Roti canai satu, kuah campur.* Then, at the end, *Bang, kira.*

Grab, from section 6: *Saya nak pergi ke Taylor's, Lakeside Campus.* as you get in, then *Terima kasih, bang* on arrival.

Campus, from section 7: *Saya pelajar antarabangsa.* Then one question — *Di mana perpustakaan?* or *Boleh saya duduk di sini?*

> [!warning]
> Do not write a sentence your phrasebook does not contain. An invented sentence is the one you will freeze on.

> [!check] Check
> Three headings, each with at least two Malay lines written out in full.

### 2. Say every line out loud twice

Read each line aloud. Twice. Your phrasebook notes that final *k* is a short stop in the throat, not a full "k" — so *tidak* comes out like "tida'". Stress is light and fairly even; many speakers lean slightly on the second-last syllable.

Say *terima kasih, bang* until the *bang* does not sound like the English word "bang".

> [!check] Check
> You can say *Roti canai satu, kuah campur* without looking at the paper.

### 3. Order at the mamak

The waiter will ask *Nak minum apa?* — what would you like to drink. Answer with your written line: *Teh tarik kurang manis, satu.*

They will then ask *Makan?* — anything to eat. Answer: *Roti canai satu, kuah campur.*

When you are done, get attention and say *Bang, kira.* They will name the price, e.g. *Empat ringgit lima puluh sen* — RM 4.50. Pay, then *Terima kasih, bang!*

> [!warning]
> If they reply faster than you can follow, say the line anyway. You are not being tested on comprehension today.

> [!check] Check
> You said at least two Malay lines and the bill arrived.

### 4. Take the Grab and use your Malay lines

Get in and say *Saya nak pergi ke Taylor's, Lakeside Campus.* When you are close, *Tolong berhenti di sini.* On arrival, *Terima kasih, bang.* Even one of these lines, said out loud to a real person, counts.

> [!warning]
> Do not let the app's English interface talk you out of saying the line. The app is not the conversation.

> [!check] Check
> *Terima kasih, bang* left your mouth, not just your head.

### 5. One campus conversation

Find one person — classmate, staff, anyone — and say *Saya pelajar antarabangsa.* Then ask your one question: *Di mana perpustakaan?* or *Boleh saya duduk di sini?*

If they answer in English, that is fine. You still opened in Malay.

> [!check] Check
> You said the introduction line to a person, not to a mirror.

### 6. Log what you abandoned

Within an hour of finishing, write three short entries: scene, phrase you said, phrase you dropped for English. Be honest — the dropped ones are the ones you will drill next week.

> [!check] Check
> Three entries, at least one of which names a phrase you did not manage.

## Checklist

**Before you call the day done**

- [ ] Three scenes attempted in one day: mamak, Grab, campus.
  - *Prevents the whole day collapsing into one scene and calling it a win.*
- [ ] At least four Malay lines spoken out loud to a real person.
  - *Written lines do not build the habit; spoken ones do.*
- [ ] The mamak order used *teh tarik kurang manis, satu* or *roti canai satu, kuah campur* — not English.
  - *They are the drink and food orders from the mamak lesson.*
- [ ] The bill was requested with *Bang, kira.*
  - *At a mamak the bill does not come until you ask for it.*
- [ ] *Terima kasih, bang* said to the Grab driver on arrival.
  - *One line to a stranger is the cheapest possible practice.*
- [ ] *Saya pelajar antarabangsa* said to one person on campus.
  - *It is the sentence that opens every future campus conversation.*

  - *The abandoned list is what you drill next week.*

  - *Turns a clumsy moment into a concrete next step instead of a vague regret.*

## Project

**One day, three scenes, one log**

Run the day described in this lesson: mamak breakfast, Grab ride to campus, one campus conversation. Then write it up.

**Constraints**
- Every Malay line you report must appear in your phrasebook, spelled as your phrasebook spells it. Do not invent sentences.
- The day must include all three scenes. A day with only the mamak does not count.
- Report what actually happened, including the lines you abandoned. A log with no abandoned lines is almost certainly not honest.

**What "done" looks like**
A short written log with three sections — one per scene. Each section names the Malay lines you said, the lines you dropped for English, and one sentence on what you would say differently next time. Finish with a three-line list of the phrases you are drilling next week.

This fits in one 15-minute sitting after the day itself: the day takes the time, the write-up takes the sitting.

Deliverables:

- [ ] A three-section log (mamak, Grab, campus) naming the Malay lines you actually said in each scene.
- [ ] A list of the phrases you abandoned for English, written out in full.
- [ ] A three-line drilling list for next week, drawn from the abandoned phrases.
