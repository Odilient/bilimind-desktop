# Numbers, money and telling the time

> [!info] Stuff to memorise · 15 min · study Tue 13 Oct
> You will be able to hear a price, count past a hundred, read a clock time and agree a day with someone. Covers satu to sepuluh, the -belas teens like sebelas, the puluh tens, kosong for the zero in phone numbers, ringgit and sen, and sekarang, hari ini, esok, pagi, petang and malam. Prevents nodding at lapan ringgit when the number you actually heard was lapan puluh.

## Explainer

You are at a mamak table. The waiter says **lapan ringgit**. You hear *lapan* and hand over RM 8. He waits. The bill was **lapan puluh** — eighty. That RM 72 gap is the whole reason this lesson exists.

Malay numbers are built from a small set of parts, and the parts are regular. Learn the ten digits and you can say any price up to 999 without new vocabulary.

| Digit | Malay |
|---|---|
| 0 | sifar (kosong for phone numbers) |
| 1 | satu |
| 2 | dua |
| 3 | tiga |
| 4 | empat |
| 5 | lima |
| 6 | enam |
| 7 | tujuh |
| 8 | lapan |
| 9 | sembilan |
| 10 | sepuluh |

Three rules generate everything else.

**Teens add *belas*.** 11 is *sebelas* (the *se-* swallows the *satu*), then 12 *dua belas*, 13 *tiga belas*, up to 19 *sembilan belas*.

**Tens add *puluh*.** 20 is *dua puluh*, 30 *tiga puluh*, 50 *lima puluh*. Units come after: 21 is *dua puluh satu*, 45 is *empat puluh lima*, 99 is *sembilan puluh sembilan*.

**One hundred and one thousand take *se-*, not *satu*.** 100 is *seratus*, 1000 is *seribu*, 1,000,000 is *sejuta*. But 200 is *dua ratus* and 2000 is *dua ribu*.

So RM 87 is *lapan puluh tujuh ringgit*. RM 8 is *lapan ringgit*. The difference between them is one word, and it is the word you must not drop.

Money: RM is *ringgit*, and 1 ringgit = 100 sen. RM 4.50 is *empat ringgit lima puluh sen*. In a shop you will hear *kosong* for the zero in a phone number or a digit string — 012 reads *kosong satu dua*.

Time is the same arithmetic. *Pukul berapa sekarang?* asks the time. *Pukul satu* is one o'clock, *pukul dua setengah* is half past two — *setengah* after the hour means half past, not half to. Days: Isnin, Selasa, Rabu, Khamis, Jumaat, Sabtu, Ahad. *Hari ini* is today, *esok* tomorrow, *semalam* yesterday. *Pagi* is morning, *tengah hari* midday, *petang* afternoon to dusk, *malam* night.

## Key points

- The ten digits satu to sepuluh are the only numbers you memorise; everything to 999 is built from them.
- Teens are formed with belas (tiga belas = 13) and tens with puluh (tiga puluh = 30).
- Hundreds, thousands and millions take se- for one: seratus, seribu, sejuta — never satu ratus.
- Kosong, not sifar, is the zero you say inside a phone number or digit string.
- 1 ringgit is 100 sen, so RM 4.50 is empat ringgit lima puluh sen.
- Setengah after the hour means half past: pukul dua setengah is 2:30, not 1:30.
- Hari ini, esok and semalam are today, tomorrow and yesterday; pagi, tengah hari, petang and malam split the day.

## Scenario

It is 9 pm at the mamak near Taylor's. You order roti canai and teh tarik. The waiter taps the bill and says a number. You are paying cash and you have a RM 10 note and a RM 100 note in your wallet.

**He says **lapan ringgit**. What do you hand over?**

- The RM 10 note.
- The RM 100 note.
- Say *Lapan ringgit?* to check, then hand over the RM 10 note.

> [!example]- Outcomes
> - ✓ The RM 10 note.: He takes it and brings you RM 2 change. The bill was RM 8 — *lapan ringgit* is eight.
> - ✗ The RM 100 note.: He looks at the note, then at you, and asks *Ada duit kecil?* — do you have a smaller note? You have just asked a mamak at 9 pm to break RM 100 for an RM 8 bill.
> - ✓ Say *Lapan ringgit?* to check, then hand over the RM 10 note.: He nods: *Ya, lapan ringgit.* Checking costs two seconds and is never wrong when money is involved. He brings you RM 2 change.

**Next morning a classmate says the group project meets **pukul dua setengah, petang**. When is that?**

- 2:30 in the afternoon.
- 1:30 in the afternoon.
- 2:30 in the morning.

> [!example]- Outcomes
> - ✓ 2:30 in the afternoon.: Correct. *Setengah* after the hour is half past, and *petang* pins it to the afternoon. You turn up at 2:30 and the group is there.
> - ✗ 1:30 in the afternoon.: You arrive an hour early. The room is empty and you sit alone with your laptop for sixty minutes.
> - ✗ 2:30 in the morning.: You set an alarm for the middle of the night. *Petang* is afternoon to dusk, so nothing is happening at 2:30 am.

> [!summary] Debrief
> Every wrong turn here comes from the same habit: hearing the first familiar word and acting on it. *Lapan* is inside *lapan puluh*; *setengah* only means half past when it follows the hour. When a number or a time decides how much money leaves your wallet or whether you turn up, repeat it back — *Lapan ringgit?* — and let the other person confirm before you act.

## A common mix-up

> [!failure] The belief
> Malay numbers are irregular like English — eleven, twelve and thirteen share nothing, so I just have to memorise each number separately.

**Why it seems right.** English really is irregular at exactly this range: eleven, twelve, thirteen, twenty, thirty. If you learned to count in English by memorising, you assume every language works that way, and the first few Malay numbers you meet (sepuluh, sebelas) look like more exceptions.

> [!success] What is true
> Malay is regular from 11 upward. Teens are the digit plus *belas* (tiga belas = 13), tens are the digit plus *puluh* (tiga puluh = 30), and only the 'one' forms change, taking *se-*: sepuluh, sebelas, seratus, seribu, sejuta. That means once you know satu to sepuluh you can already say 11 to 99 without new vocabulary — the only genuinely irregular-looking items are the se- forms, and there are five of them.

## Checklist

**Before you close this lesson**

- [ ] You can say satu to sepuluh from memory without looking.
  - *Everything above ten is built from these ten words.*
- [ ] You can produce sebelas, dua belas and tiga belas without pausing.

  - *The se- forms are the only real exceptions and the ones most often got wrong.*
- [ ] You can say RM 87 and RM 8 out loud and hear that they differ by one word.

- [ ] You know kosong is the zero in a phone number, not sifar.
  - *Drivers and shop staff read digit strings with kosong.*

> [!question] Q4 · short answer
> A friend tells you the trip costs RM 90 and leaves at pukul lapan pagi. Write both facts back in Malay, and explain in one sentence why lapan pagi cannot be confused with lapan puluh in the price.

> [!success]- Answer
> The price is sembilan puluh ringgit (90 = sembilan puluh). The departure is pukul lapan pagi, eight o'clock in the morning. Lapan pagi is a clock hour and takes no puluh because it is a single digit 8; lapan puluh is a tens value, 80, and the word puluh is what marks it as a multiple of ten.
>
> Tens add *puluh*: *tiga puluh* = 30
>
> | Criterion | Excellent | Acceptable |
> |---|---|---|
> | Writes the price as sembilan puluh ringgit | Gives sembilan puluh ringgit and notes that puluh marks the tens place. | Gives sembilan puluh ringgit with no comment on why puluh is there. |
> | Writes the departure time as pukul lapan pagi | Gives pukul lapan pagi and states that pagi places it in the morning. | Gives pukul lapan pagi without mentioning pagi's role. |
> | Distinguishes the hour 8 from the tens value 80 | States that lapan alone is a single digit and puluh is the word that turns it into a multiple of ten. | Says the two are different numbers without naming puluh as the marker. |
