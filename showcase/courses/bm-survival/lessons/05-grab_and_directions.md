# Grab, directions and getting to campus

> [!info] A skill you do · 15 min · study Thu 15 Oct
> You will be able to book a Grab, tell the driver where you are going and ask your way on foot. Covers Saya nak pergi ke Taylor's, Lakeside Campus, Abang dah sampai? when the car is not where you are standing, and kiri, kanan and terus on campus. Prevents the silent ride where neither of you is sure the other has understood.

## Explainer

The Grab arrives. You get in. Neither of you says anything useful for eleven minutes, and you spend the whole ride hoping the pin was right.

That silence is the thing this lesson removes. It is not a vocabulary problem — you already know enough words. It is a *sequencing* problem: the ride has four moments where something must be said, and if you say them in the wrong order or skip one, the driver fills the gap with his own guess.

The four moments, in order:

1. **When he calls or texts** — tell him where you are standing, in a way he can act on.
2. **Before he moves** — confirm the destination out loud, even though the app already shows it. This is your best chance to catch a wrong pin while the car is still stationary.
3. **While moving** — give directions only if he asks or if he is clearly about to miss the turn.
4. **On arrival** — stop him at the right gate, not at the security post 400 metres earlier.

Your source phrasebook has all of these in section 6. What it does not give you is the order, and the order is what stops the silent ride.

Two things about how the words sound, from section 1 of the same phrasebook. Final *k* is a short stop in the throat, not a full "k" — so *tidak* comes out closer to "tida'". And stress is light and fairly even, leaning slightly on the second-last syllable. That matters here because *belok* and *terus* are the two words a driver has to catch over engine noise, and both are short.

One structural note before you start. On campus, the same three direction words do all the work: *kiri*, *kanan*, *terus*. You will use them on foot, in a car, and pointing at a map on someone's phone. Learn them once, here.

## Step by step

The full ride, from booking to getting out, in the order it happens: first the driver has to find you, then you confirm where you are going, then you steer and stop.

### 1. When he calls, describe where you are standing

Drivers often call rather than text. Answer, and say where you are:

> **Saya tunggu di pintu utama.**

Then add one thing he can see from the road:

> **Saya pakai baju biru.**

If you are not there yet:

> **Saya lambat sikit, lima minit.**

Swap *biru* for whatever colour you are actually wearing. The colour is the whole point — it is the only part of this that he can use from a moving car.

> [!warning]
> Do not say *Saya dah sampai* (I've arrived) if you are not yet at the pickup point: it tells him you are standing there now, and he will look for you there. If you are late, say *Saya lambat sikit, lima minit.*

> [!check] Check
> He has stopped describing where he is and started describing where you are. That is the switch you are listening for.

### 2. Ask whether he has arrived, once, if the car is not where you are

If the app says the car is here and you cannot see it, send:

> **Abang dah sampai?**

*Abang* is the friendly address for a man a bit older than you — the same *bang* you use for the mamak waiter. It is the right word for a Grab driver too.

Send it once. If he does not reply within a minute, call. Do not send it three times; a driver who is circling a one-way system cannot read while driving, and repeated messages make him stop reading altogether.

> [!warning]
> Do not walk toward where you think the car is. Stay at the pin. A moving passenger and a moving driver can circle each other for ten minutes; a stationary passenger gets found.

> [!check] Check
> You have a reply, or you have called and spoken. If neither, cancel and rebook — you are past the point where more messages help.

### 3. Get in and say the destination out loud

As you open the door or just after you sit down, say:

> **Saya nak pergi ke Taylor's, Lakeside Campus.**

This is the phrase from section 6 of your phrasebook. You are not asking a question — you are stating the destination so he can confirm it. If he repeats it back or nods, you are aligned. If he says something you do not catch, point at his phone screen and say *ini* while tapping the pin.

Say it before the car moves. A wrong pin corrected at the kerb costs ten seconds; corrected 3 km down the road it costs a fare and an argument.

> [!warning]
> Do not skip this because the app already shows the destination. A campus has several gates, and a pin dropped at the wrong one still routes somewhere.

> [!check] Check
> He has said the destination back, or nodded while looking at his own screen. If neither happened, say it again more slowly.

### 4. Give directions only when he asks or is about to miss a turn

Three words, from section 6:

| Malay | English |
|---|---|
| **Belok kiri.** | Turn left. |
| **Belok kanan.** | Turn right. |
| **Jalan terus.** | Go straight on. |

And if he overshoots:

> **Pusing balik.**

Say the word, then point. The gesture and the word together survive engine noise; either one alone often does not. Your phrasebook notes that Wikivoyage also lists *Pusing kiri* and *Pusing kanan* for left and right — if a driver uses those, they mean the same thing, and *belok* is the safer word to produce yourself.

If he asks how long:

> **Berapa lama?**

is the question, and *Jalan sesak* or *Jem* is what he will say back if the answer is bad.

> [!warning]
> Do not narrate the whole route. A driver who knows the campus better than you will find constant directions distracting, and you will run out of attention before the turns that matter.

> [!check] Check
> He has taken at least one turn you asked for. If he has ignored two, stop giving directions — he knows a route you do not.

### 5. Stop him at the right gate

When you can see where you want to get out:

> **Tolong berhenti di sini.**

Say it early enough that he can pull over legally. On a campus road that means roughly 50 metres before the point you actually want, because he needs to find a gap.

If you are not sure whether you are close enough yet, ask:

> **Dekat atau jauh?**

using *dekat* and *jauh* from the same table.

> [!warning]
> Do not wait until you are level with the gate. At that point he has already passed the turning and you will be dropped on the far side of a divided road.

> [!check] Check
> The car has stopped and he has not asked you for more money than the app quoted.

### 6. On foot, use the same three words

The direction words do not change when you get out of the car. If you are lost on campus and stop someone:

> **Di mana perpustakaan?**

Then listen for *kiri*, *kanan* or *terus* in the answer. Those three are the whole of what you need to catch. If the answer comes too fast, say the place again and point — most people will point as they repeat it, and the point is the answer.

*Di mana perpustakaan?* is from section 7 of your phrasebook; the rest of the campus words come in the campus lesson.

> [!warning]
> Do not nod at an answer you did not understand. Ask again with the place name — *perpustakaan?* — and let them point. A wrong nod sends you 200 metres in the wrong direction and you will not know until you are there.

> [!check] Check
> You can see the building, or the person has pointed at something you can see.

## Checklist

**Keep this open in the car**

- [ ] Destination said out loud before the car moves
  - *Catches a wrong pin while it is still free to fix*
- [ ] Phone answered, not ignored, when the driver calls
  - *An unanswered call is the start of the silent ride*
- [ ] One landmark given: gate name or shirt colour
  - *A driver cannot use "near the building" from a moving car*
- [ ] You stayed at the pin instead of walking toward the car
  - *Two moving people circle each other; one stationary person gets found*
- [ ] Abang dah sampai? sent once, not three times
  - *A driver reading four messages reads none of them*
- [ ] Direction given as word plus point, not word alone
  - *Belok kiri over engine noise needs the hand to land*
- [ ] Tolong berhenti di sini said about 50 m before the gate
  - *He needs room to pull over legally*
- [ ] Fare on the app matches what he asks for
  - *The quoted price is the price; anything else is a conversation*

> [!note]
> If you only do three of these, do the first, the fourth and the seventh. Those three are the ones that turn into money or a long walk when they are missed.

## A common mix-up

> [!failure] The belief
> The app already shows the driver my destination, so saying it out loud is redundant.

**Why it seems right.** The pin is on his screen, the fare is fixed, and the route is drawn. Nothing about the interface suggests there is anything left to confirm, and speaking up feels like implying he cannot read his own phone.

> [!success] What is true
> The pin is where you dropped it, which is not always where you are going. Campuses have several gates, and a pin dropped on the wrong side of a dual carriageway still routes somewhere. Saying *Saya nak pergi ke Taylor's, Lakeside Campus* before the car moves costs four seconds and surfaces the mismatch while the car is stationary. The difference it predicts: if you skip it, the first time you learn the pin was wrong is when the car stops somewhere you do not recognise, and by then you are negotiating a fare in a language you have had for two weeks.

> [!question] Q3 · short answer
> Your Grab driver is approaching a junction on the campus road and asks which way to go. You need him to turn right and then keep going. Write what you say, in Malay, and explain why you say the direction word together with a hand gesture rather than the word alone.

> [!success]- Answer
> Belok kanan. Jalan terus. The word alone is short and competes with engine noise and road noise; the phrasebook notes that a final k is only a short stop and a final r is often dropped, so a short direction word is easily lost. The gesture carries the same information through a channel that noise does not affect, so the driver gets the instruction even if he only half-hears the word.
>
> Belok kiri. / Belok kanan. | Turn left. / Turn right.
>
> | Criterion | Excellent | Acceptable |

> | Keeps the instruction to the two words rather than narrating the route | Gives exactly the two directions needed and nothing more. | Gives the two directions plus one extra phrase that does not confuse the instruction. |

## Project

**One real ride, scripted end to end**

Take one actual Grab trip you will make in the next few days — campus to a mamak, campus to KL Sentral, or a ride back to your accommodation. Before you book it, write the four things you will say, in Malay, in the order you will say them: the location line, the destination line, one direction line for a turn you expect on that route, and the stop line.

Constraints:

- Every Malay phrase must come from section 6 of your phrasebook, spelled exactly as it appears there (swapping in your own shirt colour is fine). Do not compose new Malay sentences.
- The location line must contain something specific to you: the gate you will actually stand at, or the colour shirt you will actually wear.
- The direction line must name a real turn on the route, not a generic one. Look at the map before you write it.

Then take the ride and say all four. Done means you have said all four out loud to a real driver, and you can state which one he responded to fastest.

Deliverables:

- [ ] The four phrases written in order, in Malay, with the English beside each
- [ ] A one-line note on the actual route naming the turn you used for the direction line
- [ ] Two sentences after the ride: which phrase the driver reacted to immediately, and which one you had to repeat
