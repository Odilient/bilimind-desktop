# Greeting people, introducing yourself, what to call them

> [!info] Two things you mix up · 15 min · study Mon 12 Oct
> You will be able to greet someone, thank them, apologise and introduce yourself as an international student at Taylor's. Covers Helo and Selamat pagi, terima kasih, maaf, saya against aku, anda against awak, and the kinship words pakcik, makcik, abang and kakak. Prevents calling a man old enough to be your father awak.

## Explainer

You are standing at the counter of a mamak at 8am. The man behind it is roughly your father's age. You open your mouth and out comes *awak* — the word you drilled last night for "you". His face does not change, but the transaction does: he answers you in English for the rest of the order, and you have just told him, in his own language, that the two of you are peers.

Malay has two systems running side by side, and the one you pick is a social decision, not a grammar decision.

**The neutral set** is what a stranger uses. *Saya* for I, *awak* for you when you are clearly the same age or younger, *anda* for you on signs, forms and in formal writing. For a stranger you cannot place, use a title instead of "you": *Encik* (any adult man), *Puan* (a married or older woman), *Cik* (a young woman). These are the words in your phrasebook table, and they are safe because they carry no claim about the relationship.

**The kinship set** is what Malaysians actually reach for. *Pak cik* (uncle) for a man old enough to be your father, *mak cik* (auntie) for a woman the same, *abang* (older brother) for a man a few years older, *kakak* (older sister) for a woman a few years older. These are not literal family. A mamak uncle is a *pak cik* on first meeting. The Grab driver who is 30 is an *abang*. The woman at the campus counter is a *kakak*.

The rule that keeps you out of trouble: **when you can see the person, kinship beats pronoun.** *Awak* is for someone you have already established as a peer — a classmate, someone your age who has introduced themselves. Used on a *pak cik*, it reads as a demotion. Used on a *kakak* at the faculty office, it reads as a student who has not learned where they are.

The phrasebook table in section 2 of your phrasebook gives you *Siapa nama awak?* and *Nama saya Oybek.* — note that the model sentence uses *awak*, because it is written for peer-to-peer introductions. When you introduce yourself to a *pak cik*, you swap the pronoun for the kinship word and the rest of the sentence does not move: *Siapa nama pak cik?* / *Nama saya Oybek.*

Greetings layer on top of this. *Selamat pagi* until about noon, *selamat tengah hari* around midday, *selamat petang* from mid-afternoon until dusk, *selamat malam* after dark. *Helo* works at any hour and carries no religious weight. *Assalamualaikum* is the Muslim greeting you will hear often; the reply is *Waalaikumussalam*. If you are unsure, *Helo* is never wrong.

Two more words do more work than their length suggests. *Terima kasih* is thank you; *terima kasih banyak* is thank you very much, and it is what you say to a *pak cik* who has just walked your order to the table. *Maaf* covers both sorry and excuse me; *minta maaf* is the fuller apology you use when you have actually done something — knocked a drink over, arrived late to a tutorial.

## Comparison

Four ways to say "you" in Malay. They are not interchangeable, and the deciding factor is not politeness in the abstract — it is who is standing in front of you and how you need to be read.

| | Who you can safely point it at | What it claims about the relationship | What it costs you if you get it wrong |
|---|---|---|---|
| **awak** | A classmate, a hostel neighbour, someone your own age who has already introduced themselves | We are peers; no seniority either way | On a pak cik or a counter staff member it reads as a demotion — they will switch you to English |
| **anda** | Signs, forms, ads and formal writing; in speech, someone you cannot place at all | Neutral and slightly distant; the word printed on official paperwork | Nothing breaks, but face to face it sounds like a form letter; Encik, Puan or Cik sound warmer |
| **pak cik / mak cik** | A man or woman old enough to be your parent — mamak stall, taxi, security guard, neighbour | You place them a generation above you and defer to that | Almost none. This is what a Malaysian your age would say |
| **abang / kakak** | A man or woman a few years older than you — Grab driver, senior student, campus counter staff | You place them slightly above you as an older sibling | Almost none, and it is warmer than anda |

- **awak** is best for the tutorial group chat, the friend you met at orientation, anyone who has already used your first name.

- **anda** is best for any written message where you do not know the reader. Face to face, prefer Encik, Puan or Cik.

- **pak cik / mak cik** is best for every food stall, every shop counter, every older neighbour for your entire first month.

- **abang / kakak** is best for the Grab driver, the senior at the faculty office, the barista who looks 25.

## Ordering at a mamak, three ways

The same transaction, run three times. The only thing that changes is the word for "you".

**Setup.** It is 8:15am. The stall is run by a man in his fifties. You want *roti canai* and *teh tarik*. You have learned *Siapa nama awak?* and *Nama saya Oybek.* from section 2 of your phrasebook.

**Attempt 1 — the phrasebook sentence, unchanged.**
> You: *Selamat pagi. Siapa nama awak?*
>
> Pak cik: *Selamat pagi. [in English] What would you like?*

He answered the greeting in Malay and the question in English. *Awak* told him you were addressing him as a peer, and he corrected the register by switching languages. Nothing rude happened, but you lost the exchange.

**Attempt 2 — kinship swapped in.**
> You: *Selamat pagi, pak cik. Siapa nama pak cik?*
>
> Pak cik: *Selamat pagi. [name]. Nak apa?*

*Nak apa?* is "what do you want?" — the standard next move. You have been read as a polite student, and he is now speaking Malay to you.

**Attempt 3 — the full order.**
> You: *Selamat pagi, pak cik. Saya nak roti canai satu, teh tarik satu.*
>
> Pak cik: *Boleh.*
>
> *(food arrives)*
>
> You: *Terima kasih banyak, pak cik.*
>
> Pak cik: *Sama-sama.*

**What changed between 1 and 3.** Not vocabulary — you used *selamat pagi* and *terima kasih* in all three. The single substitution of *awak* → *pak cik* is what moved the conversation from English back into Malay, and *terima kasih banyak* rather than plain *terima kasih* is what closes it. Note also that *saya* never changes: it is safe in every one of the three attempts. The pronoun that carries risk is the one for the other person, not the one for you.

## A common mix-up

> [!failure] The belief
> *Awak* is just the polite word for "you", so I should use it with everyone until I know them well enough for something more casual.

**Why it seems right.** It is the word your phrasebook table puts in the model sentence *Siapa nama awak?*, and phrasebook model sentences are usually the safe default. It also sounds softer than *anda*, which reads like a form, so it feels like the friendlier choice.

> [!success] What is true
> *Awak* is the peer word, not the polite word. It asserts that you and the person you are addressing are on the same level, and it is the correct default only for someone your own age. Addressing a *pak cik* — a man old enough to be your father — as *awak* is not a grammar error, it is a social one: you have claimed equality with him. The phrasebook's model sentence is written for peer-to-peer introductions, which is why it uses *awak*; the moment the person in front of you is a generation older, the sentence stays and the pronoun swaps to *pak cik*. The difference predicts the response: use *awak* on a mamak uncle and he answers in English; use *pak cik* and he keeps speaking Malay.

## Scenario

It is your third day. You are at the mamak near campus at 8am. The stall is run by a man in his fifties. You want *roti canai* and *teh tarik*. You have learned: *selamat pagi*, *saya*, *awak*, *pak cik*, *terima kasih*, *terima kasih banyak*, *maaf*, *minta maaf*.

**You reach the counter. He looks up. What comes out of your mouth first?**

- Selamat pagi, pak cik.
- Selamat pagi, awak.
- Helo.

> [!example]- Outcomes
> - ✓ Selamat pagi, pak cik.: He nods and says *Selamat pagi* back. He is still speaking Malay. You have been read as a polite student and the transaction is running in the language you wanted.
> - ✗ Selamat pagi, awak.: He pauses for half a second, then answers in English: *Good morning, what would you like?* Nothing rude happened, but the conversation has left Malay and will not come back.
> - ✗ Helo.: He says *Helo* back and waits. It is not wrong — *helo* is never wrong — but it gave him no signal about register, so he defaults to English.

**He asks *Nak apa?* You want to order. Which sentence?**

- Saya nak roti canai satu, teh tarik satu.
- Awak nak roti canai satu.
- Maaf, boleh cakap bahasa Inggeris?

> [!example]- Outcomes
> - ✓ Saya nak roti canai satu, teh tarik satu.: He says *Boleh* and turns to the griddle. The order is in. *Saya* is the same word you would use with anyone.
> - ✗ Awak nak roti canai satu.: He stops. *Awak nak* means "you want" — you have just told him what he wants. He repeats the question slowly, in English, to check.
> - ✗ Maaf, boleh cakap bahasa Inggeris?: He switches to English and takes the order that way. You got your food, and you spent the one Malay exchange you had this morning.

**The food arrives. He sets it down. What do you say?**

- Terima kasih banyak, pak cik.
- Sama-sama.
- Minta maaf.

> [!example]- Outcomes
> - ✓ Terima kasih banyak, pak cik.: He says *Sama-sama* and moves on. You have closed the exchange in Malay, which is what makes him remember you tomorrow.
> - ✗ Sama-sama.: He looks amused: *sama-sama* is the reply to a thank-you ("you're welcome"), not the thank-you itself. That is his line, not yours.
> - ✗ Minta maaf.: He looks up, puzzled — you have just apologised for something he cannot identify. *Minta maaf* is for when you have actually done something wrong.

> [!summary] Debrief
> Every wrong turn in this scenario was the same mistake in a different costume: reaching for a word that carries no information about the relationship. *Awak* and *helo* both leave the register blank, and a stranger fills a blank with the safest option available to them — usually English. *Pak cik* is the one word that tells him who you think he is, and it is the word that keeps the conversation in Malay. Notice also that *saya* never had to change. The risk lives entirely in the word for the other person.

> [!question] Q1 · multi select
> You are at the campus faculty office. The person behind the counter is a woman you would guess is about 30. You need to ask where the toilet is. Select all the forms of address that will not read as a mistake.
>
> - a) kakak
> - b) mak cik
> - c) anda
> - d) Puan
> - e) abang

> [!success]- Answer
> a) kakak; d) Puan
>
> *Kakak* is friendly for a woman a bit older than you, and *Puan* is the polite title for a married or older woman, so neither reads as a mistake. *Mak cik* is for a woman a generation older, *anda* belongs on signs and forms rather than face to face, and *abang* is for a man.

> [!question] Q4 · short answer
> You are squeezing past a group of people blocking the walkway at the mamak. What do you say?

> [!success]- Answer
> Tumpang lalu.
>
> *Tumpang lalu* is the phrase for squeezing past people. *Minta maaf* is a full apology for something you have done, *boleh ulang* asks someone to repeat themselves, and *tak apa* means 'never mind'.
