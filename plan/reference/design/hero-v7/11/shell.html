    Step 2 · 18 min · then “Present tense: the regulars”

    Ordering politely: vorrei, not voglio

    1/6ExplanationEditMark complete

    At the bar, Italians don’t say “I want”. They say vorrei — “I would like” — and it gets you a smile instead of a raised eyebrow. One word, and the whole morning goes differently.

    ComparisonTwo words people mix up

    When you order, these axes matter:

    ToneAt the barBest for
    VoglioBlunt — “I want”Voglio un caffè.Plans: voglio vedere Roma
    VorreiPolite — “I’d like”Vorrei un caffè.Asking anyone for anything

    IllustrationAl bar

    il cornettothe croissant la tazzinathe espresso cup la macchinathe machine

    The labels are real text — in your language, never baked into the picture.

    Role-playAt a bar in Rome

    Goal: order breakfast, politely
    BaristaBuongiorno! Cosa prende?Good morning! What will you have?
    Vorrei un caffè e un cornetto, per favore.I’d like a coffee and a croissant, please.
    BaristaCerto. Al banco o al tavolo?Of course. At the counter or a table?
    Al banco, grazie.At the counter, thanks.
    BaristaEcco a lei. Due euro e venti.Here you are. Two euros twenty.

    Goal reached — nicely done. You can keep chatting or move on.

    Speaking drillIt hears you

    Vorrei un caffè e un cornetto, per favore.

    I’d like a coffee and a croissant, please.

    /vor·REI un caf·FÈ e un cor·NET·to/

    72%heard: “vorrei un caffè e un coretto per favore”

    “Cornetto” came out as “coretto”. Lean on the rn, then the double t.

    Which one do you say to the barista?

    AVoglio un cappuccino.

    Right. Vorrei is the conditional — “I would like”. Voglio isn’t wrong, just blunt: a child at the counter.
